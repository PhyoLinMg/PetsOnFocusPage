The Pomodoro technique splits study time into short, timed blocks: usually 25 minutes of focused work, then a
5-minute break. After four blocks you take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. That's the whole method. It works for
studying because it turns "revise chemistry" (vague, endless, easy to put off) into "do one 25-minute block"
(small, finite, easy to start).

Here's how to set it up, how to plan a study day around it, and how to bend it when the standard numbers don't suit
you.

## Where the name comes from

Francesco Cirillo came up with the method as a university student in the late 1980s. He used a kitchen timer shaped
like a tomato, *pomodoro* in Italian, and each timed block became "a pomodoro". You can read his own description on
[the official Pomodoro Technique site](https://www.pomodorotechnique.com). The tomato is
optional. The timer is not: a clock that counts down is what makes the block feel real.

## The basic steps

1. **Pick one task.** Not "study", but "finish the practice questions for chapter 4" or "make flashcards for the
   lecture on cell transport".
2. **Start a 25-minute timer.** Work only on that task until it rings.
3. **If a thought pulls you away, write it down and carry on.** "Reply to Sam", "look up that formula" go on a
   scrap of paper for later.
4. **When the timer rings, stop.** Even mid-sentence. Take a 5-minute break.
5. **After four blocks, take a long break**, 15 to 30 minutes.

The stopping matters as much as the starting. Ending on time keeps the next block easy to begin, and the break is
part of the method, not a reward you have to earn.

## Setting it up for revision

### Break the subject into block-sized pieces

Before you start, spend five minutes listing what you need to cover and cutting it into pieces that fit in one or
two blocks. A chapter might become: read and summarise (2 blocks), practice questions (2 blocks), check answers and
redo mistakes (1 block). Now you know roughly how long the chapter takes, which is surprisingly calming.

### Use active work inside the blocks

A timer won't save passive rereading. Fill your blocks with things that make you produce something: practice
questions, past papers, explaining a topic out loud, writing flashcards and then testing yourself on them. Save the
low-energy jobs, such as tidying notes or making a revision plan, for the last block of the day.

### Make breaks actual breaks

Five minutes goes fast. Stand up, get water, look out of a window, stretch. Scrolling your phone is the break most
people reach for, and it's the one most likely to turn five minutes into twenty. If you can, leave the phone where
it is. Our guide on [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/)
has more on that.

## A sample study day

Here's what three hours of revision might look like with the standard Pomodoro numbers:

| Time | What |
| --- | --- |
| 9:00 | Block 1: chapter 4 summary |
| 9:25 | 5-minute break |
| 9:30 | Block 2: chapter 4 summary, finished |
| 9:55 | 5-minute break |
| 10:00 | Block 3: practice questions |
| 10:25 | 5-minute break |
| 10:30 | Block 4: mark answers, redo mistakes |
| 10:55 | Long break, 25 minutes |
| 11:20 | Block 5: flashcards for chapter 5 |
| 11:45 | 5-minute break |
| 11:50 | Block 6: test yourself on the flashcards |

That's six blocks, two and a half hours of real focus, with breaks that keep your head clear. Many people find four
to eight blocks a day is plenty for serious study. If you're doing more than that, check you're still working
actively and not just sitting at the desk.

## When 25 minutes is the wrong length

The 25/5 rhythm is a starting point, not a rule. Some honest adjustments:

- **Can't get started at all?** Try 10 or 15 minutes. Starting is often the hardest part, and a short block gets you
  over the line.
- **Always mid-flow when the timer rings?** Try 40 or 50 minutes with a 10-minute break. Deep work like essay
  writing or long maths problems often needs a longer run-up.
- **Tired by the third block?** Make the long break longer, or stop after three. Pushing through usually means a
  worse fourth block, not a better day.

We go into this in more detail in [how long should a focus session be?](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/)

feel endless again.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning blocks but not tasks.** "Four pomodoros of biology" is still vague. Name what each block is for.
- **Treating an interruption as the end of the block.** If something pulls you away for a minute, go back and
  finish. If it's a real interruption, restart the block later rather than half-counting it.
- **Counting blocks instead of learning.** Six blocks of rereading is worse than three blocks of practice questions.
  The timer measures effort, not progress.
- **Studying in bed.** Your brain links places with activities. A desk, a library table, even the same corner of
  the kitchen each day helps your focus switch on faster.

## Using a Pomodoro app

Any timer works, including the one on your phone. The catch with a phone timer is that the phone is right there,
and checking it "just for a second" is how a block gets lost. A focus timer app can help in two ways: it runs the
work and break cycle for you, so you never have to set the next timer, and it can make leaving the timer feel like
a choice rather than a reflex.

Pets on Focus does this with a pet who sits with you while the timer runs. You can run a single session from 5
minutes to 4 hours, or a Pomodoro run of work cycles, short breaks and a long break, with the lengths set in
Settings. Leave the app for more than 10 seconds and the session ends, so the phone stays a timer rather than a
distraction. Finish, and your pet celebrates.

## Common questions

### How long is a Pomodoro?

A standard Pomodoro is 25 minutes of focused work followed by a 5-minute break. After four Pomodoros you take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

### How many Pomodoros should I do when studying?

Four to eight Pomodoros a day is plenty for most students, which is about two to three and a half hours of focused study. Quality matters more than the count, so fill each block with active work like practice questions.

### Can I change the 25-minute length?

Yes. Try 10 to 15 minutes if starting is hard, or 40 to 50 minutes for work that needs a long run-up, such as essays or maths. Keep the breaks whatever length you choose.

## In short

- Pick one clear task, set 25 minutes, work only on that, then take 5 minutes off.
- After four blocks, take a 15 to 30 minute break.
- Fill blocks with active work: questions, flashcards, explaining out loud.
- Change the lengths if they don't suit you, but keep the breaks.
