To get back into work after a holiday, don't try to clear everything on day one. Spend the first 30 to 45 minutes sorting your inbox and choosing three tasks, then work in short focus blocks of 15 to 25 minutes with real breaks. Plan a lighter first day, and give yourself a few days before judging how you're doing.

Feeling slow, scattered or a bit flat after time off is common. Your routine has dropped away, your inbox has grown, and your brain is still halfway on the beach. This guide gives you a plan for the night before, the first morning and the first week.

## What should you do before your first day back?

Make the first morning easier the evening before. Small preparation means you open your laptop to a plan instead of a pile.

- **Go to bed at your working-week time.** If you can, shift your sleep a night or two earlier, so the alarm isn't a shock.
- **Set out what you need.** Bag, charger, water bottle, a clear desk.
- **Write one line:** "Tomorrow I start with ___." Pick something small and clear, not the hardest job on your list.
- **Check your diary.** If a packed meeting morning is waiting, see whether anything can move to later in the week.

Don't read your whole inbox the night before. It spoils your evening and doesn't get any of it done.

## How do you handle the inbox pile?

Triage the inbox in one timed pass, then act on only the urgent items. You are sorting, not answering everything.

1. **Set a timer for 30 to 45 minutes.** A fixed limit stops triage turning into a morning of replies.
2. **Scan for urgent and time-sensitive items first.** Anything with a deadline this week, or from a person waiting on you.
3. **Sort the rest into three groups:** reply today, reply this week, no reply needed.
4. **Archive or delete what is finished.** Old newsletters and "for your information" messages don't need reading.
5. **Write the replies that take under two minutes now.** Leave longer ones as tasks.

If your messages are in more than one place (email, chat, a shared tracker), triage them one at a time rather than hopping between them.

## How do you choose what to work on first?

Pick three tasks for the day: one that is urgent, one that is easy, and one that moves something important forward. Three is enough on a first day back, and finishing all three feels like momentum.

A simple order:

1. **Start with the easy one.** A quick win breaks the "I can't remember how to do this" feeling.
2. **Do the urgent one next**, while your energy is still fresh.
3. **Put the important one last or tomorrow.** Today is for warming up, not for your hardest thinking.

## How long should focus blocks be on the first day back?

Shorter than usual: 15 to 25 minutes of focus on one task, then a five-minute break. Your attention takes a little while to rebuild after time off, and a short block makes starting easier and the end always near.

- **Day one:** 15 to 20 minute blocks, three or four of them, with room for catch-up chats.
- **Days two and three:** try 25 minutes, the classic Pomodoro length, if it feels comfortable.
- **By the end of the week:** go back to your usual length.

Use a break to stand, drink water or look out of a window rather than checking your phone. [What to do in a Pomodoro break](/guides/what-to-do-in-a-pomodoro-break/) has ideas that rest you properly.

## How do you protect your first week?

Treat the first week as a ramp, not a sprint. A few boundaries stop the backlog taking over your evenings.

- **Keep your normal finishing time.** Staying late once for something urgent is fine. Staying late every night to clear the backlog turns a holiday glow into tiredness by Thursday.
- **Book a catch-up, not a meeting marathon.** A short chat with your team or manager about what changed while you were away is worth more than reading every thread.
- **Leave space.** If you can, keep a block or two in your diary empty for the work that took longer than you expected.
- **Do your hardest thinking when you are sharpest.** For many people that is the morning; notice your own pattern. [Deep work: how to plan a session](/guides/deep-work-session-how-to-plan/) helps you protect that time once you're back up to speed.
- **Take a proper lunch.** It is the easiest way to reset an afternoon. If you work from home, [a real lunch break](/guides/lunch-break-when-working-from-home/) has a simple routine.

## What if you still feel flat after a few days?

Give it a few days. A slow, slightly reluctant week after time off is a normal adjustment, and it usually lifts as your routine returns.

If you feel dread, exhaustion or low mood for weeks, that is more than a post-holiday dip. It may point to workload, sleep or something at work worth talking through. Speak to your manager, and speak to a doctor if your mood or energy stays low.

## Common questions

### How long does it take to get back into work after a holiday?

For most people, a few days. The first day is often the slowest, and many feel close to normal by the end of the first week. It varies with the length of the break and how much work piled up.

### Should you check email while you're on holiday?

That depends on your job and your agreements, but a clean break helps most people return fresher. If you must check, set one short, fixed time, rather than dipping in all day.

### What should you do first on your first day back?

Triage your inbox for 30 to 45 minutes, choose three tasks, then start with the easy one. Save your hardest work for later in the day or later in the week.

### Is it normal to feel sad or unmotivated after a holiday?

Yes, it is common. A few low days is normal; if it lasts for weeks, talk to your manager or a doctor.

## In short

- Prepare the night before: earlier bed, one clear first task.
- Triage the inbox in a timed 30 to 45 minute pass, not a full read.
- Pick three tasks, and start with the easy one.
- Use 15 to 25 minute focus blocks with real breaks while you warm up.
- Keep your normal finishing time, and give yourself a few days before judging how you're doing.
