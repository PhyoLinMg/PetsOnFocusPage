The most reliable way to stop checking your phone while studying is to make checking it harder and less tempting:
put it out of reach, silence what pings, and decide in advance when you're allowed to look. Willpower alone rarely
lasts an evening. Changing the setup around you does most of the work, so you don't have to keep saying no.

Here's what helps, roughly from the easiest change to the most committed.

## Why it's so hard to leave alone

Checking your phone is a habit with a fast reward. Every check might bring a message, a like, or something
interesting, and the not-knowing is part of the pull. Studying, by comparison, pays off slowly. So when a page gets
hard or boring, your hand finds the phone almost before you've decided to pick it up.

It's also not just the checks themselves. Each one pulls your attention onto something else, and it takes a while
to settle back into the work. Psychologists call the leftover distraction
["attention residue"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002). A two-second glance can cost you a few minutes of
focus.

None of this means you're lazy. It means the phone is designed to be picked up, and you need a setup that's designed
to help you put it down.

## 1. Put it out of reach

Distance is the simplest fix and one of the most effective. If the phone is in another room, or in a bag across the
room, checking it takes a decision and a walk rather than a reflex.

- Leave it in another room while you study, if you can.
- If you need it nearby (for a timer, say), put it face down and out of arm's reach.
- In a library, keep it zipped in your bag rather than on the table.

Some research suggests even a phone sitting on the desk can take up some of your attention. A well-known 2017 study
found people did slightly worse on tests with their phone on the desk than in another room
([Ward and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1086/691462)), though later studies have found mixed results. Either way,
out of sight is easier than resisting it in plain view.

## 2. Silence what pings

Every notification is an invitation to check. For a study session:

- Turn on **Do Not Disturb** or a **Focus** mode, and allow only the people who'd need you in an emergency.
- Turn off notifications for social apps and games entirely. You'll still see everything when you open them; you
  just won't be called back every few minutes.
- Turn off badges (the red numbers on app icons). They're small, nagging reminders.

## 3. Decide when you're allowed to look

"I won't touch my phone all evening" is hard. "I'll check it in the break at 10:25" is much easier, because the
urge has somewhere to go. This is why timed sessions work so well. With the
[Pomodoro technique](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/), you study for a set block, often 25 minutes, then
take a short break. The phone can belong to the break.

A steady background sound, like [rain or a quiet café mix](/guides/best-sounds-for-focus/), can also make the room feel less empty without a screen.

Two tips for keeping breaks short:

- Set a timer for the break too.
- Pick one thing to do with the phone in the break (reply to messages, say) rather than opening whatever's there.

## 4. Write down the urge instead of acting on it

A lot of phone checks start with a thought: "I should text Mia back", "what's the weather tomorrow", "look up that
actor". Keep a scrap of paper next to you and write the thought down. Writing it means you won't forget it, so it
stops nagging you, and you can deal with it all in the break.

## 5. Make the phone less interesting

If the phone still wins, make it duller for the length of the session:

- Move social and video apps off your home screen, into a folder on the last page.
- Log out of the app you lose the most time to, so getting in takes effort.
- Try grayscale mode. A grey screen is surprisingly less tempting.

## 6. Use a tool that makes leaving visible

App blockers lock you out of chosen apps for a set time. They work for some people, but they can feel strict, and
it's easy to end up fighting them. A gentler option is a focus timer that notices when you leave and makes staying
feel worth it.

That's the idea behind Pets on Focus. You start a focus session and a hand-drawn pet settles in beside you. Stay in
the app and your pet stays happy. Leave for more than 10 seconds and the session ends without a reward, but you get a
notification the moment you leave, so a quick slip is easy to undo. It doesn't block your other apps; it just turns
"I'll only check for a second" into a choice you notice making. Finish, and your pet celebrates and you earn coins for
its room. On Android it notices you left through the Accessibility Service; [is accessibility permission safe for a focus app?](/guides/is-it-safe-to-give-a-focus-app-accessibility-permission-on-android/) explains what to check first.

## 7. Be kind to yourself about slips

You will check your phone sometimes. Everyone does. The goal isn't a perfect evening, it's fewer checks than
yesterday. If you slip, put the phone back out of reach and start the next session. A [shorter focus
session](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/) can help on days when the pull is strong: 10 or 15 minutes is
much easier to protect than an hour.

## A setup to try tonight

1. Phone on Do Not Disturb, in another room or in your bag.
2. A paper list next to you for anything you remember you need to do.
3. A 25-minute study block with one clear task.
4. A 5-minute break where checking the phone is allowed, with a timer to end it.
5. Repeat three or four times, then take a longer break.

## Common questions

### Where should I put my phone while studying?

In another room if you can, or zipped in a bag out of arm's reach. The further away it is, the more checking it becomes a decision instead of a reflex.

### Does Do Not Disturb help with studying?

Yes. Do Not Disturb or a Focus mode stops notifications from calling you back to the phone, while still letting chosen contacts reach you in an emergency.

### Are app blockers better than a focus timer?

It depends on you. App blockers lock chosen apps for a set time and suit people who want a hard limit. A focus timer that notices when you leave, like Pets on Focus, is a gentler option for people who find blockers too strict.

## In short

- Distance beats willpower: put the phone out of reach.
- Silence notifications and badges while you study.
- Give the urge a time: check the phone in planned breaks.
- Write down thoughts instead of acting on them.
- If you slip, start the next session. Fewer checks than yesterday is a win.
