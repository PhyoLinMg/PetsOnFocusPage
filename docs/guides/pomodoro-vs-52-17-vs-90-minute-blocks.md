Pomodoro, 52/17 and 90-minute blocks are three ways to split work into timed focus blocks with breaks. Pomodoro is 25 minutes of focus and a 5-minute break. The 52/17 rule is 52 minutes of focus and a 17-minute break. A 90-minute block is one long run of deep work followed by a 15 to 20 minute rest. None of them is proven best: pick the shortest one that keeps you in flow, and use the others when the work demands it.

Here is what each method is, where its numbers came from, and how to choose.

## What is the difference between Pomodoro, 52/17 and 90-minute blocks?

The difference is how long you work before you stop, and how long you rest.

| Method | Focus | Break | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pomodoro | 25 minutes | 5 minutes (15–30 after four rounds) | Starting tasks, revision, admin, tired days |
| 52/17 | 52 minutes | 17 minutes | Steady work that needs a run-up: writing, coding, problem sets |
| 90-minute block | 90 minutes | 15–20 minutes | Deep, complex work, once you can already focus for long stretches |

All three share the part that matters: a defined stretch of work, then a defined stop. The numbers are different, the structure is the same.

## What is the Pomodoro technique?

The Pomodoro technique is 25 minutes of work on one task, then a 5-minute break, repeated, with a longer break after four rounds. Francesco Cirillo developed it as a student in the late 1980s using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. The 25 minutes was a practical choice rather than a measured optimum.

Its strength is the low cost of starting. "Just one block" is easy to agree to, so it works well when you are avoiding a task. Its weakness is interruption: if your work takes ten minutes to load into your head, a 25-minute block gives you only a short stretch of real depth before the timer rings. The full routine is in [how to use the Pomodoro technique for studying](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/).

## What is the 52/17 rule?

The 52/17 rule says to work for 52 minutes, then rest for 17. It comes from a 2014 analysis by DeskTime, a company that makes time-tracking software, of its most productive users. Their tracked computer time clustered around 52 minutes of activity followed by about 17 minutes away.

Treat it as a pattern, not a law. The data was observational: it described what highly rated users happened to do, not what would make anyone else more productive. Time away from the screen is also not always time off; someone might be reading on paper or thinking. The useful takeaway is the rough shape: work close to an hour, then take a real, generous break.

## What are 90-minute focus blocks?

A 90-minute focus block is a single long session of deep work followed by a 15 to 20 minute rest. The idea leans on the "basic rest-activity cycle", a hypothesis from the sleep researcher Nathaniel Kleitman that alertness rises and falls in roughly 90-minute waves, as sleep stages do overnight.

The overnight cycle is well established. Whether a neat 90-minute cycle also runs through your working day is contested, and individual rhythms vary. So don't treat 90 minutes as a biological rule. Treat it as a reasonable upper limit for people who are practised at deep work and whose task really does reward an unbroken run, such as writing a long draft or working through a hard proof.

## Which one should you choose?

Choose by the task and by how you feel today, not by which method sounds most scientific.

### Pick Pomodoro when

- you are putting something off and need an easy way in
- you are tired, stressed or short on sleep
- the work is in small chunks: emails, flashcards, practice questions
- you are new to timed focus and building the habit

### Pick 52/17 when

- 25 minutes keeps ending just as you get going
- the work needs a run-up, like writing, coding or maths
- you can stay sharp for close to an hour and want longer, fuller breaks

### Pick 90 minutes when

- the task is one big, connected piece of thinking
- you can already do 45 to 50 minutes without drifting
- you can protect the whole block from interruptions

Ninety minutes of foggy work is worse than two clear 45-minute blocks, so go long only as far as you stay sharp. There is more on this trade-off in [how long should a focus session be](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/).

## How do you test them on yourself?

Run an experiment instead of guessing. It takes about two weeks.

1. **Week one: Pomodoro.** Use 25/5 on every study or work day. Note whether the timer rings too early, too late or about right.
2. **Week two: a longer block.** Move to 50/10, close to 52/17, on the days with your hardest work. Keep Pomodoro for admin and low-energy days.
3. **Compare.** Which week left you less tired at the end of the day? In which were you more often pulled out of flow by the timer?
4. **Try 90 minutes once**, on a morning when you are fresh and your phone is out of reach. If you finish it clear-headed, keep it for your deepest work.

Change one thing at a time so you know what helped.

## Do the breaks matter more than the numbers?

Yes. For ideas on what to do in them, see [what to do in a Pomodoro break](/guides/what-to-do-in-a-pomodoro-break/). The structure is more reliable than the exact length, and a break only works if you actually rest in it.

- **Stand up, move, look at something far away.** A walk or a glass of water beats another screen.
- **Keep your phone out of the break.** A scroll that starts at five minutes easily becomes twenty. See [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/).
- **Stop on time.** Ending when the timer rings, even mid-thought, makes the next block easier to begin.

## Common questions

### Is 52/17 better than Pomodoro?

Not necessarily. The 52/17 pattern comes from observing the habits of highly rated software users, not from a controlled test against Pomodoro. It suits work that needs a run-up, while Pomodoro suits getting started and tired days.

### Are 90-minute focus blocks backed by science?

Partly. A roughly 90-minute cycle is well documented in sleep, but whether the same rhythm governs alertness during the day is debated. Use 90 minutes as an upper limit for deep work, not as a rule.

### Can I mix Pomodoro and longer blocks?

Yes. Many people use Pomodoro on low-energy days and for small tasks, and a 50 to 90 minute block for their hardest work on a good day.

## In short

- Pomodoro is 25/5, 52/17 is 52 minutes of focus and 17 off, and a deep-work block is up to 90 minutes with a 15 to 20 minute rest.
- None is proven best; all three work through a clear start, a clear stop and a real break.
- Start with Pomodoro, move to around 50 minutes when 25 keeps cutting you off, and save 90 minutes for your deepest work.
- Test each for a week and keep whatever leaves you clear-headed.
