To track your focus time, count only the minutes you spent on one task without switching, write them down the moment a session ends, and look at the totals once a week. Three numbers are enough: how many focused minutes you did, on what, and at what time of day.

Tracking is not about proving you worked hard. It is a mirror that shows where your good hours go, so you can protect them.

## What counts as focus time?

Focus time is the time you spend on a single task with your attention on it. Time at your desk is not focus time, and neither is time in a meeting or a stretch spent half-checking your phone.

A fair rule:

- **Counts:** a timed session where you stayed on one task from start to finish.
- **Does not count:** breaks, messages, admin, and any session you abandoned or broke halfway through.
- **Your call:** a session that wobbled once. Decide the rule in advance, then stick to it, or the numbers stop meaning anything.

Counting this strictly gives smaller totals than "hours worked". That is the point. The honest number is the one you can learn from. For a sense of what a realistic daily total looks like, see [how many Pomodoros a day is enough](/guides/how-many-pomodoros-a-day/).

## What should I track?

Track three things per session, and nothing more at first.

| Field | Example | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minutes | 25 | Your total focus time for the day and week |
| Task | Chemistry notes | Shows what actually gets your attention |
| Time of day | 9:30 am | Reveals your best hours |

A fourth field is worth adding only if you will really use it: a 1 to 5 score for how clear-headed the session felt. Skip anything that makes logging feel like paperwork. The more effort tracking takes, the sooner you stop.

## How do I track focus time? Four simple methods

Pick one. You can change later.

1. **Paper tally.** A notebook or sticky note with one mark per session. It is quick and has no notifications. It gets hard to total up after a few weeks.
2. **Spreadsheet or notes file.** One line per session: date, minutes, task. Easy to add up and chart. It depends on you remembering to log.
3. **A timer that records sessions for you.** The timer is already running, so the log is a by-product. Nothing extra to remember.
4. **Automatic computer tracking.** Some tools record which apps or sites you use. They can be useful, but they measure screen time more than attention, and they raise privacy questions if an employer is involved. Check what any such tool collects before installing it.

If you are not sure which timer length to log, [how long should a focus session be](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/) compares the options.

## How often should I review my focus time?

Once a week is enough, for about ten minutes. Daily reviews tend to turn into self-criticism, and a single day is too noisy to learn from.

In your weekly review, ask:

1. **What was my total?** Compare with last week, not with a perfect week.
2. **Which days were strongest?** Look for what they had in common: sleep, start time, task.
3. **Which hours produced the best sessions?** Most people have a window or two. Put your hardest work there.
4. **Which tasks got the least time?** If something important is starved, schedule it first next week.
5. **What is one change?** Pick one only, such as "start the first session before opening messages".

## What do I do with the numbers?

Use them to set a floor and a modest target, not a score to beat.

- **Set a floor.** Choose a minimum you can hit on a bad day, such as two sessions. A small daily minimum is what keeps a habit going; [how to build a study streak](/guides/how-to-build-a-study-streak/) shows how.
- **Raise slowly.** If your average is 90 minutes, aim for 100 next week, not 180.
- **Watch for the ceiling.** If your total rises but your evening energy falls, you passed your limit. Go back a step.
- **Protect your best hours.** Move meetings and errands out of them where you can.

## What mistakes make tracking useless?

- **Counting everything.** Padding the log with half-focused time makes the data meaningless.
- **Logging later.** Memory inflates. Write it down when the session ends.
- **Tracking too many things.** Three fields are enough.
- **Using the numbers to feel bad.** A low week tells you something about the week, not about you.
- **Ignoring phone interruptions.** If they keep breaking sessions, fix that first; see [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/).

## Common questions

### How many hours of focus time per day is normal?

There is no official figure. Many people get 2 to 4 hours of real, single-task focus on a good day. Your own weekly average is a better guide than anyone else's number.

### Is it better to track by tasks or by time?

Track both: minutes for the total, and a short task label for the pattern. Time alone shows how much; the task shows what.

### Should I track my breaks too?

Not at first. Count focus time only, and just make sure you are taking the breaks.

### How long before tracking shows a pattern?

Usually two to three weeks of steady logging is enough to see your best days and hours.

## In short

- Count only finished sessions on one task, and log them as soon as they end.
- Track minutes, task and time of day. Add a 1 to 5 clarity score if you will use it.
- Choose the easiest method you will keep up: paper, a notes file, or a timer that records sessions.
- Review once a week, change one thing, and raise your target slowly.
- Use the numbers to protect your best hours, not to judge yourself.
