Single-tasking means doing one task at a time and giving it your full attention until you reach a natural stopping point. To start, pick one task, write down the outcome you want, close everything else, and work on it for a timed block of 25 to 50 minutes. Anything that pops into your head goes on a list, not into your day.

This guide explains what is really going on when you "multitask", how to single-task in five steps, and what to do when your work doesn't let you.

## What is the difference between single-tasking and multitasking?

Single-tasking is working on one thing; multitasking is usually switching quickly between several. Most of what people call multitasking is not two things at once. It is one thing, then another, then back, with a small cost each time you swap.

Two kinds of switching are worth separating:

- **Self-inflicted switching:** you open a tab, check a message or reach for your phone mid-task.
- **Forced switching:** a colleague taps your shoulder, a notification lands, someone calls.

Single-tasking mostly tackles the first kind, and gives you a way to cope with the second.

## Why does switching between tasks cost you focus?

Every switch makes you spend a little effort reloading what you were doing, and part of your attention stays behind on the last task. That leftover is called attention residue: part of your attention staying on the previous task after you have moved on.

You can feel it in small ways:

- Re-reading the same paragraph after checking a message.
- Opening a document and forgetting what you meant to change.
- Finishing the day busy, with several things half done.

The cost of one switch is small. The cost of fifty a day is a day that felt full and produced little.

## How do you single-task? A five-step routine

Single-tasking is a short routine you repeat for each block of work.

1. **Choose one task.** Not a project, a task: "write the intro", "answer the five emails from clients", "do questions 1 to 10".
2. **Write the finish line.** One sentence on what "done for this block" looks like.
3. **Clear the space.** Close other tabs and apps, silence notifications and put your phone out of reach. [How to set up a distraction-free desk](/guides/distraction-free-desk-setup/) covers a ten-minute setup.
4. **Set a timer for one block.** 25 minutes is a gentle start; 50 works for bigger tasks. Stay with the one task until it rings.
5. **Park stray thoughts.** Keep a scrap of paper beside you. When "I must reply to Sam" appears, write it down and carry on.

At the end, take a real break before you choose the next task.

## What do you do with interruptions and stray thoughts?

Write the thought down, decide later, and keep working. An interruption is only expensive if you follow it.

- **A thought or idea:** a single line on your scrap paper. Review the list at the end of the block.
- **A message from someone:** leave it unread until the break. If it truly can't wait, answer in one line and return to your task.
- **An urge to check your phone:** notice it, take one breath and look back at your finish line. [How to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/) goes deeper, and the advice works at a desk too.
- **A person at your desk:** say "Can I come and find you at 11:30?" It is polite and it usually works.

## How long should a single-tasking block be?

A block of 25 to 50 minutes suits most tasks, with a break of 5 to 10 minutes afterwards. Start shorter if focusing feels hard, and lengthen it once short blocks feel easy.

| Task | Block length |
| --- | --- |
| Admin, email, small jobs | 25 minutes |
| Writing, studying, problem-solving | 25 to 50 minutes |
| Demanding creative or technical work | 50 to 90 minutes |

## What if your job needs you to multitask?

Single-task in the gaps you control and batch the rest. Some roles, such as support or reception, are built around being interrupted. You can still protect a little:

- **Batch similar jobs.** Answer all emails in one block twice a day instead of all day.
- **Pick one protected slot.** Even a single 25-minute block each day counts.
- **Finish before you switch.** If you must change task, reach a stopping point first and note where you were, so you can come back quickly.
- **Tell people when you'll reply.** "I check messages at 11 and 3" sets expectations.

## Common questions

### Is multitasking really bad for you?

Switching between demanding tasks usually makes each one slower and more error-prone than doing them one at a time. Doing something easy alongside something automatic, such as walking while listening to a podcast, is generally fine.

### What is monotasking?

Monotasking is another word for single-tasking: doing one thing at a time. Both terms describe the same habit.

### Can you single-task all day?

Not realistically. Aim for a few protected blocks a day and let the rest of the day be flexible. Two or three good blocks are worth more than a perfect rule you drop by Wednesday.

## In short

- Pick one task, write the finish line, clear the space, set a timer and park stray thoughts.
- Blocks of 25 to 50 minutes suit most work, with a proper break afterwards.
- Interruptions cost most when you follow them; write them down instead.
- If your job is full of interruptions, protect one block and batch the rest.
