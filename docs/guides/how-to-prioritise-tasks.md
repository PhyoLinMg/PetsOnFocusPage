To decide what to work on first, ask three questions of each task: does it have a deadline soon, does it matter for something you care about, and is it the one you keep avoiding? Do the task that scores yes on the most questions. If two tie, start with the one you are avoiding, while your energy is highest.

This guide turns those questions into a five-minute method, shows two well-known tools (the Eisenhower matrix and "eat the frog") and explains what to do when everything feels urgent.

## What does it mean to prioritise tasks?

Prioritising means choosing the order of your tasks on purpose, instead of letting the loudest message or the easiest job choose for you. It does not mean doing more. It means that the first hour of your day goes on the task that matters most.

Without a choice, most people drift towards what is quick, new or noisy: an email, a notification, a small chore. These feel productive but often push the important task to the end of the day, when you are tired.

## How do you decide what to do first? Five minutes, five steps

Use this when you plan your day, ideally the evening before. Set a five-minute timer.

1. **List what is on your plate.** Write every task you might do today or this week. Do not sort yet.
2. **Mark deadlines.** Put a date next to anything with one. A task due tomorrow beats one with no date.
3. **Mark what matters.** Tick tasks that move a goal forward or that someone is counting on. Ignore how loud they feel.
4. **Mark the one you are avoiding.** There is usually one. Naming it takes away some of its power.
5. **Pick your first task.** Choose the one with the most marks. Write it at the top, as a specific action ([how to write a to-do list you can actually finish](/guides/daily-to-do-list-that-works/) shows how), and put the rest below in order.

You do not need a perfect ranking of everything. You need a clear number one, and a rough number two and three.

## What is the Eisenhower matrix?

The Eisenhower matrix is a four-box grid that sorts tasks by urgent and important. It is named after US president Dwight D. Eisenhower, who is said to have sorted his own work by what was urgent and what was important.

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Important** | Do it now | Schedule it |
| **Not important** | Delegate it, or do it quickly | Drop it |

How to use it:

- **Urgent and important:** deadlines, real problems. Do these first.
- **Important, not urgent:** revision, a long project, exercise. These are the ones that get squeezed. Give them a time in your calendar.
- **Urgent, not important:** most interruptions and some messages. Pass them on, answer briefly or batch them.
- **Neither:** scrolling, tidying for the sake of it. Drop them.

The matrix works best on a small list. With forty tasks it becomes a chore of its own, so run it on today's candidates, not your whole life.

## What does "eat the frog" mean?

"Eat the frog" means doing your hardest or most-avoided task first thing, so that the rest of the day feels easier. The phrase is a popular productivity saying: the frog is the task you would rather skip.

It works because avoiding a task costs energy all day. It is a poor fit when your hardest task needs a state you are not in yet, such as a creative task when you are half asleep. In that case, eat the frog at your best time of day rather than at the very start. ## Which method should you use?

Use the method that matches your problem.

| Your problem | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| Too many tasks, unclear order | The five-minute method above |
| Everything feels urgent | Eisenhower matrix |
| Always putting off one task | Eat the frog |
| Important tasks never get time | Schedule them in a block ([time blocking vs Pomodoro](/guides/time-blocking-vs-pomodoro/)) |

## What if everything feels urgent?

Ask what actually happens if the task waits a day. If the honest answer is "nothing much", it is not urgent, it is just present.

- **Check the real deadline.** Many "urgent" tasks have no date at all, only a feeling.
- **Ask who is waiting.** A person waiting on you raises priority; a vague sense of obligation does not.
- **Pick one anyway.** Doing one task well beats juggling five. [Single-tasking](/guides/single-tasking-how-to-focus-on-one-thing/) explains why.
- **Say no or later.** Move anything that is not due soon to a "later" list, so today stays short.

## Common questions

### What is the best way to prioritise tasks?

Pick your first task by deadline, importance and avoidance, then work on it alone. The best method is the one you will actually use each day, and a five-minute list is enough for most people.

### How do you prioritise when everything is important?

Compare deadlines and consequences. Ask what happens if each task waits a day, and who is affected. Whatever has the nearest real deadline or the biggest consequence goes first.

### Should you do the hardest task first?

Often, yes, if you have the energy for it. If the hardest task needs a clear head and you are groggy, do a short warm-up task first, then start the hard one at your best time of day.

### How often should you re-prioritise?

Once a day is enough for most people, with a longer look each week. Re-sorting every hour becomes a way of avoiding the work.

## In short

- Ask three questions: what is due soon, what matters, what am I avoiding?
- Pick one clear number one, and a rough two and three.
- Use the Eisenhower matrix when everything feels urgent, and "eat the frog" when you keep avoiding one task.
- Work on your first task alone, in a timed block.
