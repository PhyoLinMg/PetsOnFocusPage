To get meeting-free focus time, block one or two windows of 90 to 120 minutes in your calendar as recurring "busy" events, cluster your meetings around them, and tell your team when those windows are and what counts as urgent. Then use the window for one demanding task, not for email.

This guide covers how to set it up alone, how to agree it with a team, and how to make the time worth having.

## What is meeting-free focus time?

Meeting-free focus time is a block in your calendar that is kept free of meetings so you can do work that needs concentration. It can be a couple of hours each day, a morning, or a whole day each week that a team agrees on (often called a no-meeting day).

The point is simple: hard work needs an unbroken stretch. A 30-minute gap between two meetings is rarely enough, because you spend the first part getting back in and the last part watching the clock.

## How do you block focus time in your calendar?

Put focus time in your calendar like any other appointment, so nobody can book over it. Do this once, then let it repeat.

1. **Pick the hours.** Choose the part of the day when you are most alert. For many people that is the morning, but you know your own pattern.
2. **Make it 90 to 120 minutes.** That fits one deep session plus a break. If your days are crowded, start with 60.
3. **Make it recurring.** A weekly repeat on the same days is easier to defend than a one-off.
4. **Show it as busy.** Most calendar tools let an event appear as "busy" or "free". Choose busy, or colleagues' scheduling tools will still offer the slot.
5. **Name it with a task.** "Focus: draft Q4 plan" gets more respect than "Block".
6. **Decline or move clashes early.** Reply as soon as an invite lands, offering another time.

## How do you cluster meetings so focus time survives?

Group meetings together instead of scattering them across the day. Three or four meetings back to back in the afternoon leave the whole morning free; the same meetings spread out leave nothing worth using.

- Put recurring meetings next to each other where you can.
- Ask organisers whether a 25-minute or 50-minute slot would do, so meetings end a little before the hour and give you a gap.
- Keep one afternoon for catching up, so people have a place to send "quick questions".
- Say no to meetings with no agenda, or ask for a short written agenda first.

## How do you set up a no-meeting day for a team?

A team no-meeting day works when everyone agrees the rules up front. Agree these five things:

| Decision | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Which day | The day your team's meetings cluster least |
| What is excluded | Internal meetings; client calls may be allowed |
| What counts as urgent | A live problem, not "a quick sync" |
| Who can break it | One named person, with a reason |
| How long to try it | A trial of a few weeks, then ask the team |

Managers matter most. If a manager books over the day, everyone learns the rule is optional. Also make sure updates have somewhere else to go, such as a shared document or a short written note, or the meetings will simply move to the other days and make them longer.

## How do you use the time once you have it?

Use the window for one demanding task and start it in the first minute. An empty block can vanish into inbox tidying, so decide the task the day before.

1. Write the task as a small, specific outcome: "finish the first draft of section 2".
2. Close chat and email, and put your phone out of reach. [How to set up a distraction-free desk](/guides/distraction-free-desk-setup/) has a ten-minute setup.
3. Work in timed blocks. [Deep work session: how to plan one that works](/guides/deep-work-session-how-to-plan/) shows how to run a 50 to 90 minute block.
4. Take a real break between blocks. [What to do in a Pomodoro break](/guides/what-to-do-in-a-pomodoro-break/) has ideas.
5. At the end, write down where you stopped, so the next block starts fast.

If a stray thought or request appears mid-session, write it on a scrap of paper and carry on. Check messages after the block, not during it.

## How do you tell people you are in focus time?

Tell people before the block, not during it. Short, kind and specific works best.

- Set your chat status: "Focusing until 11:30. Urgent? Call me."
- Mute notifications for the window and turn off badge counters.
- Say when you will reply: "I'll answer everything by 12."
- Work from home or in a quiet room if the office is loud. [How to focus while working from home](/guides/how-to-focus-while-working-from-home/) covers the home side.

## What if you can't get any meeting-free time?

Take small, protected pieces. If your calendar is out of your hands, you can still claim 25 minutes between meetings, or the first 30 minutes of the day before anyone is online, or the last hour.

- Use timers: one Pomodoro, then back to meetings.
- Ask for one thing: "Could we keep Tuesday and Thursday mornings free?"
- Track what happens. [How to track your focus time](/guides/how-to-track-your-focus-time/) shows how, and a week of numbers makes a request easier to make.

## Common questions

### How much focus time should you have a day?

Most people can manage one to three focused blocks of 50 to 90 minutes a day. Two good blocks usually beat five tired ones.

### What is the best day for a no-meeting day?

Pick the day your team's meetings cluster least, and one that is not tied to a client or deadline. Midweek is popular, but your own pattern matters more than the weekday.

### Should focus time be in the morning?

Often, but not always. The best time is when you are most alert and when fewest people need you. Try mornings first and move it if you find you are sharper in the afternoon.

## In short

- Block 90 to 120 minutes, recurring, shown as busy, with a task in the title.
- Cluster meetings together so a long free stretch is left over.
- For a team, agree the day, the exceptions and who can break the rule.
- Start the block with one clear task, phone away, chat closed.
- If you can't get big blocks, protect small ones and track the results.
