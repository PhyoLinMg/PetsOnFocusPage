The way to build a study streak is to make the daily minimum so small that you can do it on your worst day: one short
timed session, at the same point in your day, with the same first task. Then plan what you'll do when you miss a day,
because you will, and the plan matters more than the streak.

Here's how to set it up so it lasts past the first excited week.

## Decide what counts as a streak day

A streak only works if you know exactly what earns the day. Pick a rule you can meet when you're tired, ill-ish or
busy, not one that suits your best day.

- **Too big:** "Study for two hours."
- **Better:** "One 15-minute focus session on my current subject."
- **Also fine:** "One Pomodoro" (a single work block, no more).

You can always do more. The minimum is only the line that keeps the streak alive. Most days, once you've started,
you'll carry on. On the days you don't, the streak survives anyway.

If you're not sure how long to make that session, [how long should a focus session be](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/)
walks through the options. For a streak, shorter is safer.

## Tie it to something you already do

A vague plan ("I'll study every day") leaves you deciding when, again, every day. Give it a cue instead:

- After breakfast, before you open your phone.
- When you sit down at your desk after school or work.
- Straight after dinner, before you sit on the sofa.

Choose a moment that happens daily whatever else is going on. Put what you need (book, laptop, notes) where you'll
see it at that moment, and decide the night before what the first task will be. Starting is the hard part, and a
decided first task takes most of the effort out of it.

## Make the first five minutes easy

The streak is won or lost in the first few minutes of a session. Two things help:

1. **Start with the easiest useful thing.** Re-read yesterday's notes, do three practice questions, open the document
   and write one bad sentence.
2. **Use a timer.** "Just one 15-minute block" is a smaller promise than "study". When the timer ends, you're
   allowed to stop. Often you won't want to.

## Track it where you can see it

Seeing the run of days is motivating, and so is the gap you don't want to leave. Any of these work:

- A paper wall calendar with a tick or a sticker per day.
- A notebook with one line per session: date, subject, what you did.
- An app that counts finished sessions for you.

Keep the tracking light. If logging takes longer than the session, you'll stop logging.

## Plan for the missed day

Every long streak ends sooner or later. What decides whether you get back to studying is how you treat the break.

- **Never miss twice.** One missed day is a blip. Two in a row starts to feel like a new habit. Make the day after a
  miss your smallest, easiest session.
- **Decide your restart in advance.** "If I miss a day, tomorrow I do one 10-minute session before anything else."
- **Don't punish yourself with a huge catch-up.** That makes tomorrow harder to start, which is the opposite of what
  you want.
- **Keep the old total.** Starting again at day one doesn't erase the days you did. They happened.

Illness, travel, exams and bad weeks are normal. A streak is there to serve your studying, not the other way round.
If keeping it starts to cause stress or guilt, shrink the minimum or take a deliberate rest day.

## Don't let the streak replace the learning

A streak measures showing up, not learning. Ten minutes of scrolling in front of a textbook keeps a streak alive and
teaches you nothing. Keep the session honest:

- Put your phone out of reach (see [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/)).
- Work on one named task per session.
- Use active methods, such as practice questions and explaining things in your own words, rather than only re-reading.

The [Pomodoro technique for studying](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/) is a simple structure for this:
a block of focused work, then a short break.

## Make it a little bit fun

Streaks that feel like chores tend to end. Small rewards help: a favourite drink in the session, a cosy sound in the
background, a tick on the calendar you enjoy making. A calm [rain or fireplace mix](/guides/best-sounds-for-focus/)
can turn the start of the session into a little ritual.

## A starter plan for this week

1. Pick your cue (after breakfast, at your desk, after dinner).
2. Set your minimum: one 15-minute session.
3. Tonight, write down tomorrow's first task.
4. Do the session. Tick the calendar.
5. If you miss a day, do your smallest session the next day. Never miss twice.
6. After a week, add five minutes only if the minimum feels easy.

## Common questions

### How long does it take to build a study habit?

It varies a lot from person to person, often several weeks or more. Keeping the daily minimum small and tied to the same cue each day makes the habit easier to keep while it forms.

### What counts as a study streak day?

Decide in advance, and make the bar low: for example, one finished focus session of at least 10 minutes. A small minimum you can meet on your worst day keeps the streak alive.

### What should I do if I break my study streak?

Start again the next day without making up for it. One missed day doesn't undo what you learned, and a plan for the miss matters more than an unbroken run.

## In short

- Make the daily minimum small enough for your worst day.
- Tie it to a cue you already have, and decide the first task the night before.
- Track it simply, and keep the session honest: one task, phone away.
- Expect to miss a day. Never miss twice, and restart small.
- The streak serves your studying, not the other way round.
