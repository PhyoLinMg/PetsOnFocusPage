To stop procrastinating on homework, pick one assignment, decide exactly when and where you will start, and set a timer for 10 minutes with your phone out of reach. Procrastination is usually about avoiding the feeling of starting, not about being lazy. A tiny first step and a fixed start time take most of that feeling away.

This guide explains why homework gets put off, then gives a five-step routine, fixes for the common excuses, and a worked example.

## Why do you put off homework?

Homework gets delayed because starting feels worse than the work itself. The task is vague, big or boring, so your brain offers something easier: a snack, a video, a quick look at your phone. Procrastination means choosing something more pleasant now and paying for it later.

Three things make it worse:

- **A vague task.** "Do history" has no first step. "Write the first sentence of the essay" does.
- **Perfectionism.** If it has to be good straight away, starting feels risky.
- **Easy distractions.** A phone within reach wins against a task you don't want to do.

None of this says anything about your ability. It means the setup needs changing, not you.

## How do you stop procrastinating on homework?

Use these five steps in order. Each one makes the next easier.

### 1. Pick one assignment

Choose the single piece of homework you will do first. Not the whole evening, just the first one. If you can't decide, pick the one due soonest.

### 2. Shrink the first step

Write the smallest action that counts as starting: "open the worksheet and read question 1", "write the title and one bad sentence", "copy out the first equation". The first step should take under two minutes. For more ways to shrink it, see [how to start studying when you don't feel like it](/guides/how-to-start-studying-when-you-dont-feel-like-it/).

### 3. Set a start time and a place

Decide "at 4:30, at the kitchen table" rather than "after I've relaxed". A vague "later" is where homework goes to disappear. Tell someone, or write it where you will see it.

### 4. Run a 10-minute timer

Tell yourself you only have to work for 10 minutes, then start a visible timer. You are allowed to stop when it ends. Most people carry on, because the hard part was starting. If you do stop, you still did 10 minutes, which is more than none.

### 5. Put your phone out of reach

Move it to another room or the bottom of your bag, not just face down. [How to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/) covers setups that hold up when you feel the pull.

## What if you still can't start?

Match the excuse to a fix.

| What you tell yourself | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| "It's too big." | Break it into pieces you could finish in 10–25 minutes, and only plan the first one. |
| "I don't know where to begin." | Write down what you do know, then the first question you can answer. Skip the hard one. |
| "I'm too tired." | Do 10 minutes anyway on the easiest assignment. If you are truly exhausted, sleep is a better plan than scrolling. |
| "It has to be perfect." | Write a rough version first. Rough drafts can be edited; blank pages can't. |
| "I'll do it later." | Choose the actual time now and write it down. |

If it feels bigger than homework, such as constant overwhelm or being unable to start anything, talking to a teacher, school counsellor or doctor is a good idea.

## How do you keep going once you've started?

Work in short blocks with a real break between them. The [Pomodoro technique](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/) uses 25 minutes of work and a 5-minute break, and it suits homework well because the end is always close. If 25 minutes feels like too much at first, start with 10 and build up.

When you are stuck, mark the spot and move on. Staring at one question for 20 minutes is its own kind of procrastination. Come back to it after the easier ones.

## A worked example

Maya has a maths worksheet, a short essay and some reading, and it's 4 pm.

1. She picks the maths worksheet, due tomorrow.
2. First step: open the worksheet and do question 1.
3. Start time: 4:30 at her desk, phone in the hallway.
4. At 4:30 she starts a 10-minute timer. It ends, and she's mid-question, so she carries on for a full 25 minutes.
5. She takes a 5-minute break, then starts the essay with one rough sentence.

She didn't feel motivated at any point. She just made starting small and removed the easy exit.

## Common questions

### Why do I procrastinate on homework even when I know it's due?

Because the discomfort of starting is felt now, while the consequence is later. Your brain picks the easier option in the moment. Shrinking the first step and fixing a start time reduce that discomfort.

### What is the 10-minute rule for procrastination?

You commit to working for only 10 minutes, with permission to stop afterwards. It lowers the barrier to starting, and many people find they keep going once they've begun.

### Should I do the hardest homework first?

It depends on your energy. If starting is the problem, begin with something quick and easy to build momentum. If you have plenty of energy and one task is clearly the hardest, do it before you get tired.

## In short

- Pick one assignment and write a tiny first step.
- Set an exact start time and place.
- Run a 10-minute timer with your phone out of reach, and allow yourself to stop.
- Carry on in short blocks with real breaks.
- Starting is the hard part, and it gets easier each time you do it.
