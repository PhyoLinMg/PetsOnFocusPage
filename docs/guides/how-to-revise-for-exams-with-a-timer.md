To revise with a timer, split each day into timed blocks of 25 to 50 minutes, give every block one small job, and take a 5 to 10 minute break after each. Fill the blocks with active work, such as practice questions and past papers, rather than rereading notes. The timer's job is to make starting easy and stopping honest.

Below: how to plan the blocks, which timer lengths suit which revision jobs, how to use a timer for past papers, and how to handle the last week before the exam.

## How long should a revision block be?

Start with 25 minutes of work and a 5-minute break, then adjust. Use the table as a guide, not a rule.

| Revision job | Block length | Break |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting a subject you are avoiding | 10 to 15 minutes | 5 minutes |
| Flashcards, definitions, quick recall | 25 minutes | 5 minutes |
| Practice questions on one topic | 25 to 40 minutes | 5 to 10 minutes |
| Essay plans, long maths problems | 40 to 50 minutes | 10 minutes |
| A full timed past paper | The exam's own length | At least 15 minutes |

After three or four blocks, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. This is the rhythm of [the Pomodoro technique for studying](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/). If you are unsure which length to pick, [how long should a focus session be?](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/) goes through the trade-offs.

## How do I plan a revision day around a timer?

Plan in blocks, not hours. Write each block as one topic and one job, and a day becomes a short list you can tick off.

1. **List the topics left.** Do this once at the start of the week, not each morning.
2. **Cut each topic into jobs.** For example: summarise from memory, do ten questions, mark and fix mistakes.
3. **Give each job one block.** If a job takes two, write it twice.
4. **Choose 4 to 6 blocks for a normal day.** [How many Pomodoros a day is enough?](/guides/how-many-pomodoros-a-day/) explains why more is rarely better.
5. **Put the hardest subject in your first block.** Your attention is usually freshest then.

A sample morning with 25-minute blocks:

| Block | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Write everything you remember about topic A from memory |
| 2 | Check your notes, fill the gaps in a different colour |
| 3 | Ten practice questions on topic A |
| 4 | Mark them and redo every mistake |

Notice that the first block uses no notes. Trying to recall something before you look it up is harder than rereading, and the effort is what makes it stick. Rereading feels productive but mostly tells you the page looks familiar.

## What should I do inside each block?

Do something that makes you produce an answer. Good block contents:

- Practice questions and past-paper questions, marked afterwards.
- Flashcards, said out loud before you flip them.
- A blank-page summary of a topic, then a check against your notes.
- Explaining a topic aloud as if teaching someone.

Poor block contents: highlighting, copying notes out neatly, and watching a lecture video on autopilot. Save low-energy jobs like tidying notes for the last block of the day.

## How do I use a timer for past papers?

Time past papers like the real thing, in two stages.

1. **Question by question.** Divide the exam time by the marks to get minutes per mark. A 2-hour paper with 100 marks gives about 1.2 minutes per mark, so a 10-mark question gets about 12 minutes. Set the timer for each question and move on when it rings.
2. **Full paper.** Once you can pace single questions, do a whole paper in one sitting, phone out of reach, with the exam's own time limit.

Then spend a separate block marking and fixing. Most of the learning from a past paper happens in the marking, not the writing.

## What about the last week before the exam?

Shift from learning new material to testing yourself.

- **Mix topics inside a day**, one per block, so you practise switching.
- **Do one timed paper every day or two**, and redo the questions you lost marks on.
- **Keep the breaks and the sleep.** Skipping both usually costs more than the extra blocks gain.
- **Stop at a set time.** An end to the day is part of the plan.

Put it out of reach, or give it a single job. Many revision blocks are lost to a quick check that turns into twenty minutes. ## Common questions

### How many hours a day should I revise for exams?

Most people do well with 2 to 4 hours of real focused work a day, not counting breaks. More hours only help if you stay sharp, so judge by how clear your head is in the evening.

### Is the Pomodoro technique good for revision?

It suits revision well, because it turns a big subject into small, finite blocks that are easy to start. It works best when each block holds active work such as practice questions.

### Should I revise with a timer on my phone?

A phone timer works, but the phone is also the main source of distraction. Keep it out of reach or use a timer that ends the session if you leave it, or use a separate kitchen timer.

### What if 25 minutes feels too short or too long?

Try 10 to 15 minutes if starting is hard, and 40 to 50 minutes for essays or long problems. Keep the breaks at any length.

## In short

- Revise in timed blocks of 25 to 50 minutes with a 5 to 10 minute break, and a longer break every three or four blocks.
- Give every block one topic and one job.
- Fill blocks with active work: recall from memory, practice questions, past papers.
- Time past papers by minutes per mark, then mark and fix in a separate block.
- In the last week, test yourself more and learn less, and keep the sleep.
