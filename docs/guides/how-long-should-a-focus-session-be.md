For most people, a focus session of 25 to 50 minutes followed by a 5 to 10 minute break works well. Twenty-five
minutes is the classic Pomodoro length and a good place to start. If you often reach the end mid-flow, move up
towards 50. If you struggle to start at all, go down to 10 or 15. The right length is the one you'll actually start,
and finish, again tomorrow.

Here's how to choose, with the trade-offs behind each option.

## Why there's no single right answer

Attention isn't a fixed tank that empties at a set rate. How long you can focus depends on the task, how much you
slept, how interested you are, how noisy it is, and how practised you are at focusing at all. A first-year student
starting revision and a programmer deep in a familiar codebase will want very different lengths.

What the common lengths have in common is a short, planned break. The break is what lets you come back for another
session instead of grinding to a halt.

## The common lengths

| Length | Break | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10–15 minutes | 3–5 minutes | Getting started on something you're avoiding; tired days; younger students |
| 25 minutes | 5 minutes | The classic Pomodoro. Revision, reading, admin, most everyday work |
| 45–50 minutes | 10 minutes | Writing, coding, maths: work that needs a run-up before it gets going |
| 90 minutes | 15–20 minutes | Long creative or deep work, once you're well practised |

You'll also see 52 minutes of work and 17 minutes of rest quoted a lot. It comes from a time-tracking company looking
at its most productive users, not from a controlled study, but it lands in the same place as the table: somewhere
around 45 to 60 minutes, with a proper break. For a side-by-side look at the three popular patterns, see [Pomodoro vs 52/17 vs 90-minute focus blocks](/guides/pomodoro-vs-52-17-vs-90-minute-blocks/).

## How to find your length

### Start at 25 minutes for a week

Twenty-five minutes is short enough to start on a bad day and long enough to get real work done. Run it for a week
before changing anything, so you're judging the length and not just one good or bad day. Our guide to
[the Pomodoro technique for studying](/guides/pomodoro-technique-for-studying/) walks through the full routine.

### Notice where the timer catches you

When the timer rings, ask yourself one question: was I relieved or annoyed?

- **Relieved, and you'd been drifting for a while** → try shorter, around 15 to 20 minutes.
- **Annoyed, because you were finally in the flow** → try longer, 40 to 50 minutes.
- **Neither, it just felt like a natural pause** → you've found it.

### Change one thing at a time

Change the length by 5 or 10 minutes at a time and give each change a few days. If you change the session length,
the break length and where you work all at once, you won't know which one helped.

## When to go shorter

Short sessions are not a lesser kind of focus. They are often the smartest choice:

- **When you're avoiding a task.** Telling yourself "just 10 minutes" makes starting almost free, and starting is
  usually the hard part. Many people find they keep going once the timer rings.
- **On a tired or low day.** A few short sessions beat one long session spent staring at the screen.
- **When the task is fiddly and draining**, like proofreading or data entry.
- **For children and younger students**, whose attention is still developing.

## When to go longer

Longer sessions pay off when the work has a big start-up cost. Writing, programming, solving long problems and
designing all take a while to load into your head. Stop every 25 minutes and you spend much of each session getting
back to where you were.

There's research behind that cost of switching. Organisational psychologist Sophie Leroy calls it
["attention residue"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002): when you move from one task to another, part of
your attention stays stuck on the first one for a while. Fewer switches means less residue, which is one reason deep
work suits longer blocks.

Even then, go long only as far as you can stay sharp. Ninety minutes of foggy work is worse than two 45-minute
sessions with a walk in between.

## What matters more than the length

- **One task per session.** A session with a clear target ("draft the introduction") beats a longer one with a vague
  one ("work on essay").
- **Proper breaks.** Stand up, move, look at something far away. A break spent scrolling rarely leaves you rested,
  and it's easy for five minutes to become twenty. See
  [how to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/) for ideas.
- **The same time and place.** Starting at the same desk at the same time each day makes the switch into focus
  faster.
- **Stopping on time.** Ending when the timer rings, even mid-thought, makes the next session easier to begin. Leave
  yourself a note on where to pick up.

## Common questions

### Is 25 minutes long enough to focus?

Yes, for most tasks. Twenty-five minutes is long enough to make real progress and short enough to start on a tired day, which is why it is the classic Pomodoro length.

### How long can a person focus without a break?

It varies with the person, the task and how rested you are. Many people work well in blocks of 25 to 50 minutes, and some deep work suits up to about 90 minutes, always followed by a real break.

### How long should a break between focus sessions be?

About 5 minutes after a 25-minute session and about 10 minutes after a 45 to 50 minute session. After three or four sessions, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

If focus keeps slipping whatever length you pick, see [why you can't focus on studying, and what helps](/guides/why-you-cant-focus-on-studying/).

## In short

- Start with 25 minutes of focus and a 5-minute break.
- Go shorter when starting is hard; go longer, up to around 50 to 90 minutes, when the work needs a run-up.
- Change one thing at a time and give it a few days.
- Whatever the length, give each session one clear task and take a real break afterwards.
