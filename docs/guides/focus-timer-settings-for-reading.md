For most reading, set a focus timer to 25 minutes of reading followed by a 5-minute break. Go up to 40 or 50 minutes with a 10-minute break if you are enjoying a book or working through a long chapter. Go down to 10 or 15 minutes if the text is dense or you are tired. Reading is quiet and easy to drift out of, so a timer gives it a start, an end and a reason to keep going.

Here is how to choose the settings, and how to use them so you remember what you read.

## What timer settings work best for reading?

Start with 25 minutes of focus and a 5-minute break, then adjust by 5 to 10 minutes at a time. These are starting points, not rules.

| Kind of reading | Focus | Break |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dense textbook or journal article | 15–25 minutes | 5 minutes |
| Ordinary chapter or set reading | 25 minutes | 5 minutes |
| A novel or something you enjoy | 40–50 minutes | 10 minutes |
| Struggling to start | 10 minutes | 3 minutes |

Dense text gets shorter blocks because understanding it is tiring, and a short block means you stop while you can still take things in. Easy, enjoyable reading gets longer blocks because the timer would only interrupt you. For the reasoning behind these ranges, see [how long a focus session should be](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/).

## Should you set the timer by minutes or by pages?

Set it by minutes. Pages vary too much: ten pages of a novel and ten pages of a statistics textbook are very different jobs. A time limit also removes the temptation to rush to hit a page count.

If you like a target, use both. Aim for a section in one block, and let the timer be the limit rather than the goal. When the timer rings, finish the sentence, mark where you stopped, and take your break.

## How do you set up a reading session?

Set up takes about a minute and saves time later.

1. **Pick one text and one goal.** "Read the section on supply and demand" is a goal. "Read economics" is not.
2. **Decide what you want from it.** Write one or two questions the reading should answer. They keep your eyes moving with a purpose.
3. **Put the phone away.** Leaving it out of reach is easier than resisting it. [How to stop checking your phone while studying](/guides/how-to-stop-checking-your-phone-while-studying/) has practical ways to do it.
4. **Start the timer, then start reading.** Do not wait until you feel ready.

## How do you remember what you read?

Reading and remembering are different jobs, and the break is a good place to do the second one. Many university study-skills pages teach a version of the same idea: look over the headings first, read with questions in mind, then say or write the main points without looking.

You can fit that into the timer:

- **Before the block (1 minute):** skim the headings and the first line of each section.
- **During the block:** read, and jot a few words in the margin or on paper when something matters.
- **First minute of the break:** close the book and write two or three sentences on what you just read, from memory.

If you cannot write anything, that is useful information. Read the section again in your next block, more slowly. Re-reading everything without checking yourself feels productive but tells you little about what stuck.

## What should you do in the break?

Make the break different from reading. Look away from the page and screen, stand up, stretch, get water. Many eye-care guides suggest looking at something far away for a few seconds every so often when you read for long stretches. If your eyes feel sore or your vision is blurry often, mention it to an optician or doctor.

Avoid scrolling in the break. It is another screen, it pulls you in, and it makes the next block harder to start. More ideas are in [what to do in a Pomodoro break](/guides/what-to-do-in-a-pomodoro-break/).

## What if you keep losing your place or rereading the same line?

That usually means the block is too long, or you are tired. Try these in order:

1. Shorten the block to 10 or 15 minutes.
2. Read a paragraph, then say what it was about in one sentence.
3. Change something small: sit upright, read at a desk instead of in bed, or move to a quieter spot (see [how to focus in a noisy room](/guides/how-to-focus-in-a-noisy-room/)).
4. If it is late and you are drifting, stop. A rested block tomorrow is worth more than an hour of skimming tonight.

## Common questions

### How long can you read before taking a break?

Most people read well for 25 to 50 minutes before a break helps. Dense or unfamiliar material usually suits the shorter end, and relaxed reading the longer end.

### Is the Pomodoro technique good for reading?

Yes, especially for textbooks and set reading you would otherwise put off. The 25-minute block makes starting easy, and the break gives you a moment to recall what you read.

### Should I use a timer for reading novels?

Only if it helps you read more. A longer block of 40 to 50 minutes, or a simple stop time, works better for fiction than short Pomodoros that interrupt the story.

## In short

- Start with 25 minutes of reading and a 5-minute break.
- Go shorter for dense text, longer for enjoyable reading.
- Set the timer by minutes, not pages.
- Use the first minute of each break to write what you remember.
- Look away from the page in breaks, and keep the phone out of them.
