A to-do list you can finish is short, specific and sized to the time you really have. Pick three tasks that matter, write each as a small action with a clear end, and match them to your available hours with some room to spare. Everything else goes on a separate "later" list, so today's list stays doable.

This guide shows how to write one in five steps, how to size it, and what to do with the tasks that don't get done.

## Why do most to-do lists never get finished?

Most to-do lists fail because they are wish lists, not plans. They collect everything you might do, with no sense of how long each item takes or how much time you have.

Common causes:

- **Too many items.** Twenty tasks for a day with four free hours cannot all happen.
- **Vague items.** "Sort out taxes" or "revise biology" has no obvious first step, so you put it off.
- **Mixed sizes.** A five-minute email sits beside a three-hour report, and the list hides which is which.
- **No time attached.** Nothing says when a task will happen, so it floats from day to day.

A list that is never finished teaches you to ignore it. A smaller list you do finish teaches you to trust it.

## How do you write a to-do list you can finish? Five steps

Write the list once a day, ideally the evening before, and it takes about five minutes.

1. **Dump everything first.** Write every task, errand and worry on a "later" list. This is not today's list; it is where things wait.
2. **Pick three priorities.** From the dump, choose the three tasks that would make today a good day. One can be small.
3. **Rewrite each as a specific action.** Start with a verb and say what done looks like: "Draft the first two paragraphs of the report", not "Report".
4. **Size each task.** Next to each, write a rough time: 15 minutes, 45 minutes, 2 hours. Add about a third, because tasks usually take longer than we guess.
5. **Order them.** Put the task you most want to avoid first, in the part of the day when you focus best.

Not sure which task goes first? [How to prioritise tasks](/guides/how-to-prioritise-tasks/) gives a five-minute way to decide.

If a task takes longer than a few hours, split it until the first piece fits in one sitting. [How to plan your study day](/guides/how-to-plan-your-study-day/) shows the same idea laid across a whole day.

## How many tasks should be on a daily to-do list?

Three main tasks plus a few small ones is a good default. The test is whether the sizes add up to less than your free time, not how many items there are.

A simple way to check:

| Step | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Free hours today | 4 hours of work time |
| Realistic focus time | About 3 hours, with breaks |
| Tasks sized | 1 hour + 1 hour + 45 minutes = 2 hours 45 minutes |
| Verdict | Fits, with a little spare |

If your sized tasks add up to more than your focus time, cut one now rather than at 5 pm. Cutting is part of planning, not failure. [How many Pomodoros a day is enough?](/guides/how-many-pomodoros-a-day/) can help you estimate how many focused blocks fit in your day.

## How do you make a task specific enough to start?

A task is specific enough when you could start it in the next two minutes without deciding anything. If you feel a flicker of "where do I begin?", the task is still too big.

| Vague | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| Revise chemistry | Do past-paper questions 1 to 8 on bonding |
| Sort out email | Reply to the five emails marked urgent |
| Work on presentation | Write the headline for slides 1 to 5 |
| Clean the flat | Clear and wipe the kitchen counters |

Write the first physical step if you need to: "Open the file and write one rough sentence." If getting going is the real problem, [how to start studying when you don't feel like it](/guides/how-to-start-studying-when-you-dont-feel-like-it/) has a first-five-minutes routine.

## What should you do with tasks you don't finish?

Decide on the spot: move it, shrink it or drop it. An unfinished task that rolls over automatically, day after day, is a sign it needs a decision.

- **Move it.** If it still matters, put it on tomorrow's list, with a time.
- **Shrink it.** If it keeps slipping, cut it to a smaller first step.
- **Drop it.** If it has moved three times and you still haven't done it, ask whether it matters. If not, delete it without guilt.
- **Keep a "done" list.** At the end of the day, write down what you finished. It shows you what you did, which a list of what remains never does.

A short end-of-day review of two minutes is enough: tick, move, drop, then write tomorrow's three. Once a week, a longer look helps too: see [how to do a weekly review in 20 minutes](/guides/weekly-review-in-20-minutes/).

## How do you stop a to-do list taking over your day?

Keep today's list separate from your master list, and look at the master list only when you plan. Checking a long list all day makes everything feel urgent.

- **Two lists, not one.** A "later" list for everything, a "today" list for three priorities.
- **Check the master list once.** During your evening or morning plan, not between tasks.
- **Park new tasks.** When a new job pops up mid-task, add it to "later" and carry on. [Single-tasking](/guides/single-tasking-how-to-focus-on-one-thing/) covers how to park stray thoughts.
- **Protect a start time.** Give your first task a clock time, not just "morning".

## Common questions

### How long should a daily to-do list be?

Three main tasks plus a few small ones is enough for most days. If you can't say roughly how long each will take, the list is probably too long or too vague.

### Should I write my to-do list the night before or in the morning?

The night before works well, because you wake up knowing what to do. Spend five minutes at the end of the day choosing tomorrow's tasks, and adjust in the morning if needed.

### What is the difference between a to-do list and a task list?

Usually nothing; people use the two terms for the same thing. What matters is whether the list is specific, sized and short enough to finish.

### Should I use paper or an app for a to-do list?

Use whichever you will look at every day. Paper is quick and has no notifications; an app helps if you need reminders or the list is long. Try one for a week and keep the one you actually open.

## In short

- Dump everything on a "later" list, then pick three priorities for today.
- Write each as a specific action, and size it with a rough time.
- Make the sizes add up to less than your focus time.
- Move, shrink or drop what you don't finish, and note what you did.
- Use a timer to turn each task into a block you can start.
