Timeboxing means giving a task a fixed amount of time in advance and stopping when that time is up, instead of working until it feels finished. Pick the task, decide "40 minutes", start a timer, and when it rings you stop, check what you have and decide what happens next. It is a good fit for tasks that could swallow your whole day, like polishing an essay or tidying notes.

## What is timeboxing?

Timeboxing is a time management method where the time is the fixed part and the amount you get done is the flexible part. You set the box first (say 30 minutes) and let the work fit inside it.

Most tasks have no natural end. You can always read one more article, tweak one more sentence, make the notes a little prettier. A timebox gives the task an edge. That has two effects:

- **Starting is easier.** "Work on my essay" is vague and a little scary. "Work on my essay for 30 minutes" is small and finishable.
- **Perfectionism gets a limit.** When the box ends, the task is done for now, even if it is not perfect.

The idea comes from software and project teams, but it works for one person with a notebook just as well.

## What is the difference between timeboxing and time blocking?

Time blocking plans your whole day in calendar slots. Timeboxing caps how long one task is allowed to take. People use the words loosely, and the two overlap: a timebox can sit inside a time block.

| | Timeboxing | Time blocking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | One task and a time limit | The whole day or week |
| Main question | "How long is this worth?" | "What goes where in my day?" |
| Needs a calendar | No | Yes |
| Good for | Tasks that expand to fill the time | Days with too many competing tasks |

[Time blocking vs Pomodoro](/guides/time-blocking-vs-pomodoro/) covers the planning side in more detail.

## How do you timebox a task?

Choose one task, set a limit, work until the timer rings, then review. It takes a minute to set up.

1. **Pick one specific task.** "Answer questions 1 to 6", not "maths".
2. **Set the box.** Ask how long the task is worth, not how long it could take. For most study and desk tasks, 25 to 50 minutes is a comfortable box.
3. **Decide what "done" looks like.** A rough first draft, six questions attempted, three pages read.
4. **Start a timer and work on that one thing.** Close other tabs and put your phone out of reach.
5. **Stop when it rings.** This is the part that makes it timeboxing.
6. **Take two minutes to review.** What got done? What is left? Pick one: finish it in a new box, move it to tomorrow, or let it go.

## Should the box be hard or soft?

A hard timebox ends when the timer does, and a soft one lets you extend it once, briefly, if you are close. Use a hard box when you tend to over-polish or when the next thing on your list is fixed. Use a soft box when stopping mid-thought would cost you more than it saves.

A simple rule for a soft box: you may extend once, by at most a quarter of the original time, and you say out loud what you will finish in that time. Otherwise it quietly turns into "no limit".

## How long should a timebox be?

Aim for 25 to 50 minutes for focused work and 10 to 15 for small admin jobs. Longer than about 90 minutes and the box stops being a small, finishable thing. [How long should a focus session be?](/guides/how-long-should-a-focus-session-be/) goes through how to find your own length.

Some starting points:

| Task | Box to try |
| --- | --- |
| Email and messages | 15 minutes |
| Flashcards or practice questions | 25 minutes |
| First draft of an essay section | 40 minutes |
| Tidying notes | 20 minutes |
| Reading a chapter | 45 minutes |

If you keep running out of time, your boxes are too small or your tasks too big. Split the task, or enlarge the box next time. Guessing wrong is part of learning how long things take.

## Where does timeboxing help most?

It helps most with tasks that are open-ended or put off. Try it for:

- **Procrastinated tasks.** A 20-minute box makes the start feel affordable. See [how to stop procrastinating on homework](/guides/how-to-stop-procrastinating-on-homework/).
- **Perfectionist tasks.** Set a box for the rough version, then a separate one for polishing.
- **Research and reading.** Cap how long you spend looking things up, so research does not replace the writing.
- **Planning.** Give your plan for tomorrow five minutes, as in [how to plan your study day](/guides/how-to-plan-your-study-day/).

It is less suited to work that needs long, unbroken thought. For that, plan a longer stretch with [deep work session: how to plan one that works](/guides/deep-work-session-how-to-plan/).

## Common questions

### What is an example of timeboxing?

Deciding to spend 30 minutes drafting an essay introduction, setting a timer and stopping when it rings, even if the draft is rough. You then decide whether to start a second box or move on.

### Is timeboxing the same as the Pomodoro technique?

Not quite. The Pomodoro technique is one fixed pattern: 25 minutes of focus and a 5-minute break. Timeboxing is the general idea of capping a task, and you choose the length each time.

### What if I don't finish in the time?

That is useful information. Note what is left, decide whether to start a new box or move it to another day, and set a longer box next time for a similar task.

## In short

- Timeboxing fixes the time for a task and lets the output flex.
- Set one task, one limit, a clear "done", then stop when the timer rings.
- Use hard boxes for over-polishing, soft boxes (one short extension) for work you can't drop mid-thought.
- Try 25 to 50 minutes for focused work, and review for two minutes afterwards.
